## Core thesis

Duolingo treats onboarding as a motivation problem, not a data-collection problem: it
says motivation is the hardest part of learning and designs for daily return (C1, C2).
That follows from the business model. The product is free, growth is mostly organic and
word-of-mouth, and revenue comes from converting a small share of a large engaged base,
so a first session that loses the user loses everything downstream (C3, C4, C5). The
consequence for the first run is to let people use the product before asking them for
anything: the sign-up screen was moved behind a first lesson, and the account request is
skippable several times before it becomes mandatory (C6, C7, C8), framed as saving
progress (C9). The commitment asked for is deliberately small, one short lesson a day,
because the team found a bigger goal stood in the way of the habit (C10, C11, C12). The
habit device is the streak, which openly leans on loss aversion (C13) but is given slack
on purpose, because the same posts admit that a lost streak demotivates and that fear of
loss can stop people from starting (C14, C15, C16). Everything is tested, at hundreds of
experiments per quarter (C17, C18), yet the company states limits: mission outranks
metrics, brand design is not traded for a win, spammy notifications and deceptive
patterns are named as short-term tactics, and notification volume is protected (C19,
C20, C21, C22). Tone is part of the mechanism: wording that removes fear, praise for
effort, characters and celebration animations (C23, C24, C25, C26, C27). The playbook is
not clean: Duolingo's own posts describe guilt, a "desperate" mascot and an "addictive"
widget (C28, C29, C30), so the boundary against shame-based pressure in the reference
file is drawn with an outside authority (C33), not claimed as Duolingo's practice.
